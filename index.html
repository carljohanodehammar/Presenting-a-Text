Speaking / Part one

Explain it for someone who has not read it.

You have read the text several times. Your listeners have not read it at all. This guide is about the distance between the two: how to make your message clear, your purpose visible and your context enough, and how to see while you speak whether your listeners are still with you.

An opening for listeners
This study asks a simple question: do students who sleep more get better marks? The short answer is yes, but only a little.

01 / Three appeals

Your listeners ask three silent questions.

More than two thousand years ago, Aristotle described three ways a speaker wins an audience. He called them ethos, pathos and logos. The names are old, but the questions behind them are the ones every listener still asks, usually without saying them aloud. A good presentation answers all three.

Ethos
Why should I trust you?

Trust in the speaker and the source. You build it by knowing the text, reporting it fairly, naming the author and being honest about limits, including your own.

Pathos
Why should I care?

Connection with the listeners. In academic speaking this is not drama. It is relevance: an example from their lives, a question to think about, a voice that shows the point matters.

Logos
Why is this true?

Reasons and evidence. A clear claim, the evidence behind it, and a sentence that explains why the evidence supports the claim. A clear order is part of logos too.

They work together

Logos without pathos is correct but nobody listens. Pathos without logos is manipulation, and an academic audience notices. Neither works if the listeners do not trust you. When you read a text, you can use the same three questions to judge how the author tries to convince you.

02 / One clear message

Before you plan anything, say the whole text in one sentence.

A listener hears your presentation once and cannot go back. If they remember one sentence, which one should it be? That sentence is your message. It is not the topic, which only says what the text is about. It is what the text says about the topic, and ideally why that matters.

Topic

The text is about sleep and students' marks.

Message

Students who sleep more get slightly better marks, so sleep is part of studying, not a break from it.

  1. Find the claim

    What does the author say is true, or what did the study find? Look for the verb: found, shows, argues, concludes.

  2. Keep the size of the claim

    If the text says slightly, may or in this group, your message says it too. A message that is bigger than the text damages your ethos.

  3. Add why it matters

    One short phrase that links the claim to your listeners. This is where logos meets pathos.

  4. Say it early and say it again

    Give the message near the start and repeat it at the end. In writing this would be repetition. In speaking it is help.

03 / Purpose made visible

Say what the text is trying to do, and what you are trying to do.

Every text has a purpose. It may inform, persuade, warn, or correct a belief that many people hold. Listeners understand the details much better once they know the purpose, because they know what the details are for. You have a purpose too: usually to help your listeners understand the text well enough to discuss it. Say both aloud.

04 / Enough context

Give the background your listeners need, and no more.

Context is what a listener needs to know before your message makes sense. Too little, and they are lost from the first minute: you use words and names they have never met. Too much, and the message disappears under details that only matter to someone who will read the whole text. The test is simple. Does the listener need this now, to follow what comes next?

Usually needed

The topic in plain words. Who was studied, where, and roughly how. Any key term, explained with an example. Why the question matters to these listeners.

Usually not needed

Exact dates, every number in a table, the full list of methods, the history of the field. If someone asks, you can give it then.

05 / Adapt to your audience

Turn written sentences into spoken ones.

A reader can read a sentence twice, see the headings and look up a word. A listener hears everything once, at your speed. Academic writing packs a lot of meaning into long noun phrases. Speaking unpacks it again. You do not make the ideas simpler. You make the sentences easier to follow.

06 / Is the audience with you?

Watch, check, and adjust while you speak.

A presentation is not finished when it is planned. While you speak, your listeners are telling you whether they understand, mostly without words. A good speaker looks up from the notes often enough to see this, checks with a real question, and changes something when the signs say the listeners are lost.

Signs they are with you

  • They look at you and at each other when you make a point.
  • They nod, or write something down after a key sentence.
  • They react at the right moment, for example to a surprising result.
  • Their questions build on what you said.
  • They can answer a check question in their own words.

Signs you are losing them

  • Blank faces, or eyes on phones and on the window.
  • Frowning at a word or a number.
  • Nobody reacts where you expected a reaction.
  • They ask about something you explained a minute ago.
  • Silence after Any questions?

  1. Watch

    Look up after every main point. Look at every listener, not only the friendliest face.

  2. Check

    Do you understand? almost always gets a polite yes. Ask something that needs a real answer: Which of the two results surprised you more?

  3. Adjust

    Slow down. Say it another way. Give an example. Repeat the main point. Cut a detail if time is short.

07 / Put it together

Write the first minute of a presentation.

The first minute carries most of the weight. It gives the context, the message and the purpose, and it decides whether the listeners trust you enough to keep listening. Read the invented study card. Then write what you would say in your first minute to listeners who have not read the text. Write it as you would say it, not as you would write an essay.

Include: enough context, the message in one sentence, the author's purpose, your own purpose, and one question that checks understanding.
Where this leads

From a clear message to a clear route.

Much of this guide rests on reading you have already practised. Finding a writer's position, audience and purpose is where Reading Strategies 1 began, and giving a fair opinion of a text, anchored in something the text contains, is the core of Source Evaluation 2. Speaking adds one new demand: everything has to work the first time it is heard.

The next step is the route through your presentation. Listeners cannot see your headings, so you have to say them: where you are, where you are going and when you have arrived. The companion page, Signposting Language, is a phrasebank you can practise with, memorise from and use to build your own notes card.

Until then, one question before every presentation. If my listeners remember only one sentence, which one will it be?
